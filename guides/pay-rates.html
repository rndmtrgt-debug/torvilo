WORKER GUIDE

Understanding Australian pay rates

Your legal minimum is not always one universal hourly number. The rate that applies can depend on the award or registered agreement covering the work, your classification and your employment circumstances.

Start with what covers your job

For most Australian employees, minimum pay is set by an award covering their industry or occupation. A registered agreement can instead set pay and conditions for a workplace. If no award or registered agreement applies, the National Minimum Wage may apply.

If you are unsure, check your employment documents and ask your employer, payroll or HR team what award or agreement they are using. The Fair Work Ombudsman also provides tools for finding applicable minimum rates.

Classification matters

An award can contain several classifications with different minimum rates. The appropriate classification can depend on the duties you perform, your level of responsibility, qualifications or other criteria in the award. Finding the name of an award is therefore only part of checking a rate.

Ordinary rates, penalties and allowances

Your base hourly rate may not be the only amount relevant to a shift. Depending on the applicable award or agreement, different provisions can apply to weekends, public holidays, overtime, particular working times or work that attracts an allowance.

This is why TORVILO's Shift Pay Calculator asks you to enter the rate and multiplier you want to model rather than trying to automatically choose a legal rate for every worker.

Casual, junior, apprentice and trainee rates

Employment type and personal circumstances can affect minimum rates. Casual employees can have a casual loading, while different rules or rates can apply to juniors, apprentices and trainees. Check the provisions that apply to your own employment rather than assuming another worker's hourly rate applies to you.

Current context (September 2026): The National Minimum Wage is $26.44 per hour or $1,004.90 per week from 1 July 2026 for employees not covered by an award or registered agreement. Most employees are covered by an award, so check the rate that applies to you.

Worked example: checking a mixed-rate payslip

Suppose your own award or agreement confirms a $32 ordinary hourly rate and a $48 rate for a particular category of work. If a pay period contains 30 paid ordinary hours and 6 paid hours in that other category, the gross arithmetic is (30 × $32) + (6 × $48) = $1,248. Paying all 36 hours at $32 would instead give $1,152, a difference of $96. The rates in this example are hypothetical: the example does not establish an entitlement to a particular penalty or loading.

What to write down before checking your pay

  1. The date and pay period you are checking.
  2. The award or registered agreement, plus the relevant classification.
  3. Whether you were casual, part-time or full-time for those hours.
  4. Paid hours in each applicable category, excluding unpaid breaks.
  5. The applicable rates, their effective dates and any allowances.

How to verify a rate

Use the Fair Work Ombudsman minimum wages information and official pay guides. If an enterprise agreement applies, check the agreement itself as well.

Keep your own shift records

Knowing the correct rate is easier to use when you also know what hours you worked. Record dates, paid hours and relevant shift details, then compare them with your payslip. TORVILO's Shift Tracker can help you keep a simple local record, and How to check your pay explains a practical comparison process.

Pay rates and award provisions can change. Always verify current official information for the period you are checking.

A practical rate-check sequence

Start with the date the work was performed, because rates can change over time. Identify the award or agreement, then the classification and employment type. After that, check whether the hours fall into ordinary time or another category such as overtime, weekend or public-holiday work. Only once those inputs are verified should you use a calculator to reproduce the arithmetic.

Why comparing with a co-worker can mislead

Two employees can perform similar work but have different classifications, employment types or contractual arrangements. A colleague's hourly rate can be a useful prompt to investigate, but it is not proof of the minimum that applies to you.

Keep the date with the rate

If you are checking older payslips, use the rate that applied then rather than today's rate. Save the relevant pay guide or note its effective date alongside your records. That makes a later pay check reproducible instead of relying on a current figure that may have changed.
