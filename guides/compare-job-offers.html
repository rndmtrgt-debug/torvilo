WORK & MONEY GUIDE

How to compare two job offers in Australia

A bigger advertised salary does not always mean a better hourly return or more money left over after work-related costs. Compare offers on the same basis before deciding.

1. Compare base pay, not just the headline package

Ask whether each figure is a base salary, an hourly rate or a total remuneration package that includes employer super. If a package of $84,000 includes 12% super and no other components, the implied base salary is $84,000 ÷ 1.12 = $75,000. By contrast, an advertised $80,000 base salary plus super has a higher cash salary, even though its headline number may appear similar.

Super arrangements and what counts as ordinary time earnings can vary. Ask the employer to confirm the breakdown in writing rather than relying on a conversion alone.

2. Compare the hours you are actually expected to work

Consider two hypothetical offers. Offer A pays $75,000 base for 38 paid hours each week. Offer B pays $82,000 base but regularly requires 45 hours each week. Assuming 52 paid weeks, Offer A works out to approximately $37.96 per hour ($75,000 ÷ 1,976 hours). Offer B works out to approximately $35.04 per hour ($82,000 ÷ 2,340 hours). The larger salary has a lower effective hourly return under these assumptions.

This comparison is an arithmetic planning exercise, not a determination that extra hours are lawful or unpaid. Review the contract, relevant award or enterprise agreement and any overtime provisions. Use the annual-to-hourly calculator to test your own hours.

3. Put recurring work costs on the same time scale

Compare additional travel, parking, tolls, uniforms and equipment you personally pay for. For example, an extra $12 in commuting costs per workday across 230 commuting days is $2,760 per year. A $3,000 increase in gross salary would not automatically mean $3,000 more in take-home pay, so consider tax as well. These costs are a household-budget comparison, not a statement about tax deductibility.

4. Compare leave and predictability

Permanent full-time and part-time employment may provide paid leave entitlements that are not equivalent to casual employment. A casual hourly rate can include casual loading but may not include paid annual or personal leave. Consider the likely paid weeks per year, how stable the roster is, and whether public holidays, weekends or nights affect your rate. Read our employment types guide.

5. Look beyond the pay packet

Record the location, travel time, training, roster flexibility, job security, advancement opportunities and any probation conditions. A shorter commute or more predictable hours can be valuable even if the nominal pay is slightly lower. Don't put a made-up dollar value on benefits that matter personally: note them separately.

Job-offer comparison checklist

  • Is the advertised amount base salary, hourly pay or a package including super?
  • What are the contracted and realistically expected weekly hours?
  • Are overtime, penalties, allowances and bonuses guaranteed or conditional?
  • What paid leave applies, and how many weeks will actually be paid?
  • What will commuting and other unreimbursed work costs be?
  • How predictable are shifts, location and roster changes?
  • What does the written contract say about notice, probation and pay reviews?
